Research prospectus · Working document

Measuring the Price of Hindsight

A point-in-time FX research database, and an experiment to decompose backtest contamination into its constituent sources

Status: Phase 0 of 7 complete · Path C (intraday and multi-day, narrow scope) · Last revised {{ captured_at }}

Abstract

A backtest is a claim about a counterfactual: had this rule been followed then, this is what would have happened. The claim holds only if the simulation is confined at every instant to information that existed at that instant. In practice it rarely is, and the failure is silent — a contaminated backtest raises no error and produces no anomaly, only better results.

That look-ahead bias exists is not in dispute. What is missing is arithmetic. Practitioners can list the ways a backtest leaks the future; almost nobody can say what any one of them is worth. This project builds a foreign-exchange research database in which the point-in-time guarantee is enforced by executable tests rather than by care, then uses it to run one pre-registered rule under four data regimes that differ only in what the simulation is permitted to know. The differences between the four results are the estimand. The output is not a strategy; it is a price list for hindsight, denominated in Sharpe units.

1 The problem

Consider the United States employment report for January 2024. On the morning of 2 February 2024 the Bureau of Labor Statistics announced that non-farm payrolls stood at 157,700 thousand. That number moved markets within milliseconds. It was also wrong — not in any culpable sense, but in the ordinary sense that early estimates are built from incomplete survey returns and are superseded as returns arrive.

By March the figure was 157,533. By April, 157,560. The annual benchmark revision in February 2025 moved it to 157,049, and the next moved it to 157,032. The published value fell by 668 thousand over two years, a distance roughly three times the monthly change that traders were reacting to in the first place.

Now suppose a researcher tests a rule that trades the payrolls surprise. If the database returns 157,032 for January 2024 — the current value, the one a routine query returns — then the simulated trader in February 2024 is acting on a figure that would not be published for two more years. The strategy is not clever. It is clairvoyant, and the backtest cannot tell the difference, because at no point does anything fail.

This is look-ahead bias. It has several distinct channels, and they are usually discussed as one undifferentiated hazard:

  • Revision leakage. Using a value that had not yet been published, as above.
  • Timestamp coarsening. Storing when something became known to the day rather than the second, so a strategy may act at 09:00 on news released at 09:30 — or at the prior day's close on news released the following morning.
  • Cost understatement. Simulating fills at a mid price, or at an assumed constant spread, when the real cost is the bid-ask spread prevailing at that moment — which widens precisely during the news events a macro strategy trades.
  • Universe leakage. Selecting instruments, date ranges, or parameters with knowledge of how the sample turned out.

Each is individually well known. What is not known — and what this project sets out to measure — is how much each one is worth in a controlled setting where everything else is held fixed.

2 Why the problem persists

The persistence of look-ahead bias is not primarily a matter of carelessness. It is produced by four structural features of ordinary data practice, each of which is correct in its own domain.

2.1 Databases are built to store the present

The dominant convention in data engineering is that a table holds the best current value of a fact. When a value changes, the row is updated and the prior value ceases to exist. This is right for nearly every application — an address book should hold your current address, not a history of where you have lived — and it is catastrophic for research into decisions made in the past. The default is not merely unhelpful here; it actively destroys the record that research requires.

2.2 Vendors serve revised series by default, without warning

The standard public interfaces to macroeconomic data return the current series for the whole history. A researcher who downloads a CSV of payrolls receives today's values stretching back to 1939, with nothing indicating that the early portion was published years after the dates it describes.

This project verified a specific instance. The keyless CSV endpoint at the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis accepts a vintage_date parameter and silently ignores it: four requests specifying vintages in 2005, 2015, 2024, and none at all returned byte-identical payloads (SHA-256 177efe81de346565), each carrying data through 2026. Asking explicitly for the January 2005 vintage returns today's numbers, with no error and no warning. Vintage access exists, but only behind an authenticated interface most casual users never reach.

The failure mode this project studies is not hypothetical. It is available on request, for free, and it looks exactly like success.

2.3 Revisions are not noise

It is tempting to treat revisions as zero-mean measurement error that will wash out in a long sample. It does not. Aruoba (2008) documents that revisions to major US macroeconomic series are biased, are predictable from information available at the time of the initial release, and fail the properties that "well-behaved" measurement error would have. Revisions carry systematic structure related to the business cycle, so a strategy tested on revised data is not merely advantaged — it is advantaged more in some regimes than others, and precisely in the regimes where macroeconomic strategies live or die.

2.4 The bid-ask spread is inconvenient to store and expensive to ignore

Storing separate bid and ask series roughly doubles the volume of a tick archive, and most freely available price histories therefore publish a mid price or a single "close". But the spread is the transaction cost, it is not constant, and it widens sharply at exactly the moments a news-reactive strategy wants to trade. A backtest that assumes a fixed spread does not make a small conservative error; it makes an error concentrated entirely in the trades it depends upon.

3 Why it is worth solving

3.1 The canonical demonstration

The strongest existing evidence that real-time data changes conclusions rather than merely refining them comes from monetary economics. Orphanides (2001) re-examined Taylor-rule characterisations of US monetary policy using the data policymakers actually had at each meeting rather than the revised series available to later researchers. The substantive conclusions changed. Policy that appears mistaken with hindsight can appear reasonable given contemporaneous information, and estimated rules differ materially.

That result is the template for this project's claim. Orphanides did not show that real-time data is a hygiene improvement. He showed that using it or not determines the answer. This project asks the analogous question for trading strategy evaluation, and — unlike Orphanides, who was answering a substantive economic question — treats the size of the discrepancy as the object of interest in its own right.

3.2 The replication problem in empirical finance

Empirical asset pricing has a well-documented reliability problem. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that, given the number of factors tested against the same data, conventional significance thresholds are far too permissive and a large share of published factors are likely false discoveries. Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020) attempt to replicate several hundred documented anomalies under uniform methodology and find that a majority do not survive. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu (2014) show how readily backtest overfitting manufactures impressive out-of-sample-looking results from noise.

Multiple testing is the usual explanation, and it is a real one. But it is not the only one, and the two are confounded in the existing literature: a result that survives correction for multiple testing may still rest on data the strategy could not have had. Isolating the data-contamination component requires holding the strategy fixed and varying only the information regime — which is exactly the design in §9.

3.3 The practical stake

Capital is allocated on the strength of backtests. The difference between a reported Sharpe ratio of 1.5 and a realisable one of 0.3 is not an academic curiosity; it is the difference between a strategy that funds a desk and one that quietly loses money for two years before being cut. Any tool that converts "your backtest is probably optimistic" into "your backtest is optimistic by approximately this much, from these sources" changes the conversation from an argument about diligence into an argument about a number.

4 Research question and contribution

Holding the trading rule, the instrument, and the sample fixed, how much of a reported backtest result is attributable to each individual source of information contamination?

The intended contributions are three, in decreasing order of novelty.

  1. A decomposition, not a binary. The literature treats point-in-time discipline as a methodological requirement that a study either satisfies or does not. This project treats each contamination channel as a quantity with a measurable price, reported in Sharpe units and basis points.
  2. An enforcement mechanism rather than a convention. The database is accompanied by an acceptance suite written before the pipeline, against an empty schema, and expected to fail. Point-in-time correctness becomes a property that is continuously tested rather than a claim made in a methods section.
  3. A measured account of free FX data quality. Two independent feeds are ingested and reconciled, and the disagreement rate is published rather than resolved. The distribution of tick pathologies by hour and instrument is a secondary empirical output.

5 Why this design and not another

Several routes lead to a defensible answer. The choice among them was driven by what each would leave the researcher able to say afterwards.

ApproachWhy not
License a commercial point-in-time database Fastest route to clean vintages, and the wrong instrument for this question. It requires paid registration, and more importantly it relocates the guarantee into a vendor's black box. One would be measuring contamination while trusting an unexamined claim that the control arm is uncontaminated — and would learn nothing about the mechanism being studied.
Simulate revisions synthetically Tractable and fully controlled, but it presupposes the answer. Revision processes would have to be modelled, and Aruoba (2008) shows real revisions are biased and predictable in ways a plausible noise model would not reproduce. The magnitudes are empirical, so synthetic data can only return the assumptions fed into it.
Careful coding without enforcement The defining property of this failure is that it is silent. A convention that depends on the researcher remembering it, in a codebase they will return to in six months, is not a control. This is why the acceptance suite precedes the pipeline rather than following it.
Study equities instead A far larger cross-section and better statistical power, but dominated by confounds this project is not trying to study: survivorship bias, delisting returns, corporate actions, index reconstitution. Isolating revision timing amid those would be a different and harder paper. Free equity tick data with genuine bid and ask is also not available.
A single "correct" backtest, reported honestly The obvious approach, and it yields one number with nothing to compare it to. A Sharpe of 0.31 is uninterpretable alone. The contribution comes entirely from the differences between regimes, which requires running the contaminated variants deliberately.

The last row is the design's central move and its most counter-intuitive: the project deliberately constructs backtests it knows to be wrong. The dishonest variants are not strawmen to be dismissed — they are the measuring instrument. Each is a faithful reproduction of a mistake that appears in real research, and the gap it opens against the honest variant is the quantity being estimated.

6 Why foreign exchange

FX is an unusual choice for a study of macroeconomic data contamination, and the reasons are practical rather than doctrinal.

  • Free tick data with separate bid and ask actually exists. This is rare and it is decisive. Measuring the cost of the mid-price assumption requires having the real spread to compare against. Most free price histories make that impossible by construction.
  • Macroeconomic releases are scheduled to the second and dominate short-horizon moves. The identification is clean: a known instant, a known surprise, a liquid instrument reacting immediately.
  • The market trades continuously from Sunday evening to Friday evening. There is no overnight close, so the timestamp-coarsening effect can be measured without an overnight-gap confound contaminating it.
  • The cross-section is small. Roughly eight major pairs, against thousands of equities. For a study whose experimental variable is the data regime rather than the instrument, a narrow universe reduces the multiple-testing surface inside the experiment itself.

The small cross-section is also a genuine cost, and it cuts the opposite way from intuition. Fewer instruments means less statistical power, which makes overfitting risk higher, not lower. Multiple-testing correction matters more in this setting, not less — and any strategy-level finding here is correspondingly weaker than the same finding in equities would be.

One structural concession is unavoidable. There is no consolidated tape in foreign exchange: no canonical price, only each venue's own view. Every result is therefore conditional on the feed that produced it. This is why a second independent feed is ingested and the disagreement rate published rather than reconciled — it converts an unacknowledgeable weakness into a measured quantity.

7 How the project took its present shape

The project began from a narrow observation about asymmetry.

Detecting look-ahead bias in someone else's work turns out to require only two questions: did you use the first published value or the revised series? and did you enter at the release timestamp or at a daily open? Both take seconds to ask. The answers are usually involuntary — a researcher who says "I pulled it from FRED" has answered the first question without realising a question was asked.

The asymmetry is that evaluating the answer is much harder than asking the question. Told that revisions exist but are too small to matter for a given series, one has no basis to agree or disagree without knowing the magnitudes. For payrolls they run to the hundreds of thousands; for some series they are genuinely negligible. The published literature establishes that revisions are consequential for inference (Orphanides, Aruoba) but not what they are worth to a simulated trading result, which is the currency in which the argument is usually conducted.

That gap defined the project. Building a point-in-time database was never the objective; it is the apparatus required to run the measurement. The design followed from asking what the smallest system capable of producing that number would be — which is why the scope is deliberately narrow, why strategy code is confined to the final phase, and why the acceptance tests were written before any pipeline existed.

An early decision was to choose among three research horizons: intraday, multi-day carry, or both at reduced scope. Multi-day carry was rejected on a hard constraint — forward points have no free source, and the available proxy (policy-rate differentials) carries an error correlated with funding stress, which is exactly when carry strategies matter. Building a study whose central input is knowingly broken at the decisive moments was not defensible. The intraday horizon was adopted with a narrowed instrument universe.

8 Method

8.1 The bitemporal principle

Every fact carries two independent time axes. ref_period is the period the value describes; known_at is the instant it became publicly available. A point-in-time query filters known_at <= t and retains the latest known_at per key, returning what was believed at t rather than what later proved true. Revisions then require no special handling — they fall out of the filter.

Two properties are load-bearing. Both axes must be present: a table carrying only ref_period is not point-in-time and cannot be made so retroactively, because the information was never recorded. And known_at must be a timestamp with time zone, never a date — an 08:30 release and an 08:31 price are distinct objects, and collapsing them destroys the very effect §9 sets out to measure.

8.2 Storage

Three tiers, because the data has two incompatible shapes. Reference data and macro vintages are relational, small, and require integrity constraints (PostgreSQL). Tick data is append-only, immutable, and projected at billions of rows (ClickHouse). The research query layer is in-process and reads from both (DuckDB). The single most important architectural rule is that as_of(t) in the query layer is the only sanctioned read path; convenience accessors that reach past it into the storage tiers are how contamination re-enters a system that had eliminated it.

8.3 Raw data is immutable; cleaning is additive

Ticks are never modified after ingestion and never transformed during it. Quality detectors write to a separate flag table rather than correcting or deleting rows. A detector later found to be wrong is corrected by deleting its flags and re-running, not by re-acquiring several hundred gigabytes. This also preserves the pathologies themselves as an object of study: the distribution of crossed quotes, stale quotes and spread outliers by hour is a secondary result rather than debris.

8.4 Verification precedes construction

Four families of acceptance test — no-clairvoyance, revision, tick sanity, cross-feed — were written against an empty schema before any pipeline code, and currently fail. The red suite is the intended state: each test states a guarantee the system does not yet provide, and each failure is required to be attributable to the guarantee being absent rather than to an unrelated defect.

This functions as the project's analogue of pre-registration. The tests specify what correct behaviour looks like at a moment when there is no implementation to rationalise around, which removes the possibility of tests that encode what the pipeline happens to do rather than what it must do.

9 The experiment

One rule, pre-registered before results are examined, evaluated under four data regimes that differ only in what the simulation may know. The candidate rule is a directional position in EURUSD held for a fixed horizon of several minutes following a US CPI or payrolls release, conditioned on the sign of the surprise.

The rule is deliberately dull. It is not expected to be profitable and its profitability is not the object of measurement — an interesting rule would invite tuning, and tuning would reintroduce exactly the selection effects the design exists to isolate.

VariantMacro valueTimingExecution cost
A — HonestFirst print, as of release Exact release timestampPrevailing bid/ask
B — RevisedFinal revised value Exact release timestampPrevailing bid/ask
C — Revised, date-onlyFinal revised value Date only; entry at day openPrevailing bid/ask
D — Revised, date-only, midFinal revised value Date onlyMid price, no spread

Each successive variant adds one advantage that was unavailable in reality, so that each adjacent difference isolates a single channel:

  • A → B prices revision leakage.
  • B → C prices timestamp coarsening.
  • C → D prices the mid-price assumption.

Variant A is the control and the only regime corresponding to information a trader could have acted upon. The estimand is the set of differences, not the levels; the level of A is of little interest and is not expected to be attractive.

10 Expected results

These are hypotheses recorded in advance, not predictions offered with confidence. Several may prove wrong, and being wrong is a legitimate and reportable outcome. They are stated here so that confirmation bias is harder to exercise later.

IDHypothesisConfidence
H1 Reported Sharpe ratios order D > C > B > A. Each variant strictly adds an information or cost advantage unavailable in reality. High
H2 The mid-price assumption is the single largest contributor to the A→D gap, because the rule is short-horizon and trades in a window where spreads widen. Moderate
H3 Timestamp coarsening (B→C) exceeds revision leakage (A→B). Strongly dependent on holding period; likely reversed at multi-day horizons. Moderate to low
H4 Cross-feed disagreement is non-trivial and clusters around rollover, releases and thin holiday sessions. High on existence, low on magnitude
H5 The share of ticks carrying a quality flag is higher than commonly assumed and concentrates in identifiable hours. Moderate
H6 The two-to-three week windows in which US and EU daylight-saving transitions are misaligned show measurably different session-overlap behaviour. High that the artefact exists; unknown whether it is economically meaningful

H2 is the hypothesis most exposed to falsification and therefore the most informative. If revision leakage dominates the spread effect instead, that inverts the usual practitioner intuition that execution assumptions are the primary source of backtest inflation, and should be reported prominently rather than buried.

11 Limitations

The following are structural. They cannot be engineered away within the project's constraints, and they are stated here rather than left to be discovered by a reader.

  1. No consolidated tape. There is no canonical FX price. The primary feed is one venue's aggregated view, and another broker's tape will differ, especially in the tails and around rollover. Every result is conditional on this feed.
  2. Reported volume is not market volume. The volume field reflects the provider's own pool. Nothing is built on it.
  3. Small cross-section. Roughly eight majors. Less statistical power, hence higher overfitting risk, and multiple-testing correction matters more rather than less.
  4. No free historical consensus forecasts. Surprise measures must be constructed from revisions or from a synthetic forecast, and that choice affects results. Where a synthetic forecast is used it is labelled as such.
  5. No forward points. Any carry extension must proxy with policy-rate differentials, and covered interest parity has failed persistently since 2008 (Du, Tepper and Verdelhan, 2018), so the proxy error correlates with funding stress.
  6. Vintage timing is date-granular at source. Archival macro sources record the date a value became known, not the time. Release times must be joined from published agency schedules, and where a time cannot be sourced it is recorded as explicitly unknown rather than assumed.
  7. Retail execution assumptions. Even with genuine bid and ask, the data does not capture slippage, rejection, requoting or last-look behaviour. Simulated fills remain optimistic by an unmeasured amount — which means variant A is itself a conservative upper bound on honesty, not a perfectly clean control.

The last point deserves emphasis. This project measures the distance between four degrees of contamination; it does not claim that the least contaminated of them is uncontaminated. The absolute level of variant A should not be read as an achievable result.

12 What this project does not claim

It does not produce a profitable trading strategy, and is not intended to. It is not a trading system: there is no execution, no order management, and no live component. It does not establish that any particular published result is contaminated. And it does not claim that its own control arm is free of look-ahead bias — only that its three comparison arms are contaminated in specified, deliberate, and individually measurable ways.
